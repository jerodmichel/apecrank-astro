<p class="western" style="line-height: 100%; margin-bottom: 0in;"><span style="color: #333333; font-family: inherit; font-size: large;">The
following is a program that introduces Mandarin Chinese to the
English-speaker. The discussion of language is also accompanied by a
brief introduction to the well-known sixth-century essay, <i>Thirty-six</i><i>
Stratagems </i><span style="font-style: normal;">(with historical
examples and illustrations)</span>, as well as their corresponding
hexagrams from the t<span style="text-decoration: none;">he
ancient Chinese philosophy of change, the </span><i><span style="text-decoration: none;">Yijing</span></i><span style="text-decoration: none;">.
The structure is such that each chapter contains one of the 36
stratagems as a kind of theme to which the vocabulary, character</span><span style="text-decoration: none;">s</span><span style="text-decoration: none;">,
and sample sentence</span><span style="text-decoration: none;">s</span><span style="text-decoration: none;">
have been related, </span><span style="text-decoration: none;">and
each chapter is denoted by the hexagram corresponding to that
stratagem. </span></span>
</p>
<p class="western" style="line-height: 100%; margin-bottom: 0in; text-decoration: none;">
<span style="color: #333333; font-family: inherit; font-size: large;"><br /></span>

</p>
<p class="western" style="line-height: 100%; margin-bottom: 0in;"><span style="color: #333333; font-family: inherit; font-size: large; text-decoration: none;">Concerning
the origin of the material comprising the language portion, the
author’s first year in China was partially spent learning Mandarin
(speaking, reading and writing) in the following way: everyday they
would go out and, not knowing much Mandarin at all, would come into
situations where knowing how to say some certain words would have
been a great help, so they made a list of these words (20 apprx.)
and, with the help a friend and colleague, memorized how to read
write and pronounce them; the following day they went out again and,
using the words from the list, ran into yet more situations
compelling them to make a new list. After doing this weekly for about
a year, their Mandarin had greatly improved. The above-mentioned
method produced a very natural and unique progression of lists, and
it is of these that the program is comprised. It is also worth
mentioning that the language content is completely scaffolded in the
sense that, whenever a new character, word or phrase is introduced,
the reader is shown how to read, write and pronounce it, and nowhere
will a character, word or phrase appear unless it was already
previously introduced.</span></p>
<p class="western" style="line-height: 100%; margin-bottom: 0in;"><span style="color: #333333; font-family: inherit; font-size: large;"><br /></span>

</p>
<p class="western" style="line-height: 100%; margin-bottom: 0in;"><span style="color: #333333; font-family: inherit; font-size: large;">We
will assume some familiarity with pronunciation and reading pinyin,
as well as a handle on how to write some basic characters. Having
said this, the reader should also know that this program is certainly
introductory, and would be most useful to beginners who are
independent and who enjoy reading and learning.</span></p>

<span style="color: #333333; font-family: inherit; font-size: x-large;"><b>A Brief Introduction to
<i>Yijing</i> (the Chinese Philosophy of Change)</b></span></p>
<p class="western" style="line-height: 100%; margin-bottom: 0in; text-decoration: none;">
<span style="color: #333333; font-family: inherit;"><br /></span>

</p>
<p class="western" style="line-height: 100%; margin-bottom: 0in; text-decoration: none;">
<span style="color: #333333; font-family: inherit;"><br /></span>

</p>
<p class="western" style="line-height: 100%; margin-bottom: 0in; text-decoration: none;"><span style="color: #333333; font-family: inherit; font-size: 1.2em;">
</span><span style="color: #333333; font-family: inherit; font-size: large;">The ancient Chinese philosophy of change, based on the <i>Yijing</i>
(Book of Changes), is comprised of three distinct layers. Its fist
layer consists of eight trigrams, together with the 64 hexagrams
obtained by taking all possible pairs of the trigrams; it believed by
many to have been created by Fu Xi (<span><span lang="zh-CN">伏羲</span></span>)
himself, the mythical First Emperor of China, born in the
twenty-ninth century B.C. Its second layer consists of the names of
and descriptive statements associated with each of the hexagrams,
allegedly written down by King Wen and the Duke of Zhou during the
eleventh century B.C. Its third layer consists of seven writings,
making up the "Ten Wings", composed between the fifth and
second centuries B.C., the authors of which used the hexagrams to
describe the cosmic patters that relate humanity to nature.</span></p>
<p class="western" style="line-height: 100%; margin-bottom: 0in; text-decoration: none;">
<span style="color: #333333; font-family: inherit;"><br /></span>

</p>
<p class="western" style="line-height: 100%; margin-bottom: 0in; text-decoration: none;"><span style="color: #333333; font-family: inherit; font-size: 1.2em;">
</span><span style="color: #333333; font-family: inherit; font-size: large;">The philosophy of change underlying the <i>Yijing</i> treats the
cosmos as one grand self-generating organismic process, without
beginning or end, like a great motion in which "all of the parts
of the process belong to one whole", and where each part
interacts spontaneously with the others.</span></p>
